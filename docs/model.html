The model

Two small neural networks, an actor that drives and a critic that judges, learning together from millions of frames of their own driving. There are no human laps to copy: everything comes from trial, error and the score.

What it is

How it learns

1. Drive. 8 cars drive 256 frames each on random tracks: 2,048 frames with what the actor did and what it scored.

2. Judge. For each frame the critic guessed the score still to come. The advantage is how much better things went than that guess: positive means "that action was better than usual here". It uses the actual rewards a few frames ahead and the critic's guess after that (GAE, λ = 0.95, a future reward 100 frames away counts 0.99100 ≈ 37%).

3. Improve. The actor makes actions with a positive advantage more likely and those with a negative one less likely; the critic moves its guesses toward what really happened.

A2C vs PPO. A2C, the original algorithm of this project, takes one gradient step on each batch and throws it away. PPO takes 10 passes over the same batch, but clips each change, so the new policy can't drift more than ~20% from the one that collected the data. Same network, same advantages; PPO gets ~10× more learning out of every frame. Both are in a2c_agent.py (--algo a2c / --algo ppo).

What it is paid for

CarRacing's own score, and nothing else: each of the track's ~300 tiles pays 1000/N the first time a wheel touches it (about +3.3), and every frame costs −0.1. A full lap in 800 frames scores about 1000 − 80 = 920. Going fast is worth it, but a missed tile is gone, and leaving the 2,000-unit playfield costs −100 and ends the episode. In the first stage an episode is also cut short (−50) after 1 s fully on grass or 3 s without a new tile.

To keep learning steady, rewards are divided by the running spread of the discounted return (the critic then predicts numbers near 1, not 300). When an episode hits the 1000-frame limit, the critic's value of the last state is added, because a time limit is not the car's fault.
